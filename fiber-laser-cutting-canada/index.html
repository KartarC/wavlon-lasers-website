Buyer's Guide

Buying a Fiber Laser Cutter in Canada

What actually matters when you specify a machine — choosing laser power, sheet versus tube, the electrical work nobody mentions until installation day, and what "Canadian support" should mean on paper.

On this page

  1. Choosing laser power
  2. Sheet cutting or tube cutting
  3. What's different about buying in Canada
  4. The Wavlon range at a glance
  5. Cost, financing, and payback
  6. What support should include

A fiber laser cutter is usually the largest single capital purchase a fabrication shop makes. The specification decisions you make up front — power, bed size, enclosure, automation — determine what work you can quote for the next decade. This guide covers the choices that matter most, and the ones that are easy to get wrong.

1. Choosing laser power

Laser power is the first number everyone asks about, and the most common place to overspend. Higher wattage does two things: it raises the maximum thickness you can cut, and it cuts thin material faster. If your work is mostly thin gauge, extra kilowatts buy you speed rather than capability — and speed only pays if you have the order book to keep the machine loaded.

A practical way to size the machine: take the thickest material you cut regularly, not the thickest you have ever cut. One-off thick jobs are usually cheaper to outsource than to buy capacity for.

Watch the duty cycle, not just the peak. A machine rated for 40mm will cut it — but slowly, and with high gas consumption. If thick plate is a daily activity rather than an occasional one, size up rather than running the machine at its ceiling.

2. Sheet cutting or tube cutting

These are different machines, not settings on the same machine. A flat-bed sheet cutter works on plate held on a slatted bed. A tube cutter holds the workpiece in rotating chucks and cuts as it turns, which is what allows cope cuts, saddle joints, and angled end profiles on structural sections.

If you are currently bandsawing tube and then grinding fit-up by hand, a tube laser usually removes more labour than a second sheet machine would. Weld prep that used to be a manual operation comes off the machine ready to assemble.

Choosing between the two tube platforms

  • TubeCut Double Chuck — 1.5–12kW, sections to 360mm, 6,000mm bed. Two synchronized chucks. The practical entry point for most shops.
  • TubeCut Triple Chuck — 6–30kW, sections to 680mm round, 12,000mm bed, loads to 1,500kg. The third chuck supports the workpiece through the cut, which is what holds accuracy on long, heavy structural profiles.

The deciding factor is usually section size and length rather than power. If you are running 12-metre structural steel, bed length settles the question before wattage does.

3. What's different about buying in Canada

Most fiber laser specifications are written for other markets. Three things reliably catch Canadian buyers out:

Electrical supply

Canadian industrial shops commonly run 600V three-phase, while many machines arrive configured for other standards. This has to be sorted before the machine ships, not discovered on installation day. Every Wavlon machine is pre-configured for Canadian electrical and safety standards before it leaves, and we coordinate with your electrician on power requirements ahead of delivery.

Currency and financing

A machine quoted in US dollars exposes you to exchange-rate movement between quote and delivery — a real cost on a purchase this size, and one that is rarely mentioned in the sales conversation. Financing in Canadian dollars removes that risk entirely.

Who actually shows up

This is the one that matters most and is hardest to verify before you buy. Ask directly: who performs the installation, where is the technician based, and what is the response commitment when the machine is down? A machine that is cheaper up front but waits weeks for an overseas technician is not cheaper.

A useful question to ask any supplier: "If this machine stops on a Tuesday morning, what happens, and who do I call?" The specificity of the answer tells you most of what you need to know.

5. Cost, financing, and payback

Purchase price is only part of the operating picture. When you compare quotes, account for assist gas consumption, consumables such as nozzles and protective windows, electrical supply work, and whether the machine needs a water chiller and the plumbing to serve it.

Most buyers finance rather than paying outright, which keeps working capital available for materials and labour. Through our equipment finance partners we offer leases, loans over 36–72 months, and a 90-day deferred payment option for qualified buyers — so the machine can be installed and producing before the first payment falls due. Terms run from 24 to 84 months, $0 down is available with approved credit, and approval is often within 48 hours.

Because financing is arranged in Canadian dollars, there is no exchange-rate exposure between approval and delivery. See financing options →

6. What support should include

Support is the part of the purchase that is easiest to promise and hardest to check. As a baseline, a Canadian buyer should expect all four of the following in writing:

  • On-site installation and commissioning — rigging, levelling, alignment, calibration, and first-part test cuts, with the technician not leaving until the machine produces parts to spec.
  • Operator training at your facility — machine operation, software, nesting, maintenance routines, and safety, included with the purchase rather than sold separately.
  • Warranty on parts and labour — Wavlon machines carry a standard 2-year warranty across all machine systems, with extended 3-year and 5-year plans available.
  • Remote diagnostics — secure remote access so software faults and configuration problems are resolved without waiting for a site visit.
